Lesson 05

Copyright & Creative Commons

Use other people's work the right way

You're working on a school project about robots. You find the perfect image on Google, copy it into your presentation, and submit it. That's fine, right? Wrong. The person who took that photo owns it, and using it without permission is like stealing their work. In 2023, a Thai student was sued for using a photographer's image without credit on their blog. In another case, a student was expelled for downloading and sharing copyrighted movies on the school network. But here's the good news — there's a world of free, legal content out there called Creative Commons. Creators share their work for you to use, as long as you give them credit. Today, you'll learn to use other people's work the right way — and protect yourself from legal trouble.

📚 What You Need to Know

What is Copyright?
Copyright is a legal right that protects original creative works. When someone creates something — a photo, a song, a video, a piece of writing, or software — they automatically own the copyright to that work. This means no one else can copy, distribute, or use that work without permission.
What Copyright Protects:
Type of WorkExamples
Literary WorksBooks, articles, poems, essays
Musical WorksSongs, compositions, sheet music
Artistic WorksPaintings, photographs, sculptures
Dramatic WorksPlays, scripts, screenplays
Audiovisual WorksMovies, videos, TV shows
Sound RecordingsMusic recordings, podcasts
SoftwareApps, programs, code
Architectural WorksBuildings, designs
How Long Does Copyright Last?
  • ⏳ Generally, copyright lasts for the life of the creator + 70 years
  • ⏳ After that, the work enters the public domain
Real-World Example: A musician writes a song. Without permission, no one can use, copy, or sell that song. If someone uses it in a YouTube video without permission, the musician can file a copyright claim to have the video removed or monetized.
What is Fair Use?
Fair use is a legal doctrine that allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission for specific purposes. It's a defense against copyright infringement.
When Fair Use May Apply:
PurposeExample
EducationUsing a short clip in a classroom presentation
CriticismQuoting a book in a review
CommentaryUsing a movie clip to analyze film techniques
News ReportingShowing a photo in a news story
ParodyCreating a funny version of a song
ResearchIncluding a diagram in a scientific paper
Real-World Example: A student uses 10 seconds of a movie clip in a presentation to analyze cinematography. This is fair use because it's for education, uses only a small portion, and doesn't harm the movie's market.
What is Creative Commons?
Creative Commons (CC) is a licensing system that allows creators to share their work with specific permissions. Instead of "all rights reserved" (traditional copyright), Creative Commons offers "some rights reserved."
The Six Creative Commons Licenses:
LicenseWhat You Can DoRequirements
CC BYUse, share, modifyMust give credit to the creator
CC BY-SAUse, share, modifyCredit + share under the same license
CC BY-NDUse, shareCredit + NO modifications
CC BY-NCUse, share, modifyCredit + NON-COMMERCIAL only
CC BY-NC-SAUse, share, modifyCredit + non-commercial + share under same license
CC BY-NC-NDUse, shareCredit + non-commercial + NO modifications
Where to Find CC-Licensed Content:
  • 📷 Unsplash — Photos (free with attribution)
  • 📷 Pexels — Photos, videos (free with attribution)
  • 📷 Pixabay — Photos, videos, music (free with attribution)
  • 📷 Flickr — Photos (filter by CC license)
  • 🔍 Creative Commons Search — All types (various CC licenses)
Real-World Example: A photographer uploads their photo to Flickr with "CC BY 4.0" license. Anyone can use, share, and modify the photo as long as they credit the photographer. A student uses the photo in their project and writes: "Photo by John Doe, Flickr, CC BY 4.0"
What is Public Domain?
Public domain works are not protected by copyright and can be freely used by anyone for any purpose. No permission is needed, and no credit is required (though it's always good practice to credit).
How Works Enter the Public Domain:
  • ⏳ Copyright expired
  • 🎯 Creator dedicated the work to the public domain
  • 🏛️ Works created by the US government
  • 📄 Facts, ideas, and titles are not copyrightable
Real-World Example: Shakespeare's plays are in the public domain. Anyone can adapt, perform, or sell them without permission. That's why you see so many modern versions of "Romeo and Juliet" and "Hamlet."

✅ Examples for Students

Example 1 — Correct & Incorrect Use
❌ Incorrect✅ Correct
Downloading a Disney image and using it in a school commercial project without permissionSearching on Pixabay for a free-to-use image of a castle and citing: "Castle by Pixabay, free for commercial use."
Using a song from YouTube in a video without creditUsing a song from FreeSound with CC license and crediting the artist
Copying a paragraph from Wikipedia without citationParaphrasing the information and citing Wikipedia as the source
Example 2 — Finding CC-Licensed Content
  1. Go to https://search.creativecommons.org
  2. Search for "space exploration"
  3. Select a photo with CC BY license
  4. Download the image
  5. Add attribution: "Space Shuttle by NASA, CC BY 2.0"
Example 3 — Understanding CC Licenses

🧪 Activity: Find & Cite a CC-Licensed Image

Let's Get Started!
Part 1: Research
  • Students are given a topic (e.g., "climate change," "coral reefs," or "space exploration")
  • Search on Creative Commons, Unsplash, or Pixabay
  • Find one image that is licensed under CC BY or CC BY-SA
Part 2: Download and Cite
  • Download the image
  • Write proper attribution: "Title by Author, Source, License"
  • Example: "Coral Reef by MarineBio Society, Flickr, CC BY 2.0"
Part 3: Presentation
  • Create a short slide or poster using the image
  • Present to the class, explaining what license the image has, how you credited the creator, and why it's important to respect copyright
