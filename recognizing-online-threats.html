Digital Safety

Recognizing Online Threats

Spot the bait before you bite

You receive an email that looks exactly like it's from your school. It has the school logo, the principal's name, and a professional design. It says: "Your account has been compromised. Click here immediately to verify your identity or your account will be permanently deleted within 24 hours." The logo looks real, the language sounds official, and you're scared. But there's one problem — it's a trap. This is called phishing, and it tricks millions of people into giving away their passwords, credit card numbers, and personal data every single year. In 2024, phishing attacks increased by 40% worldwide, and teenagers are one of the most targeted groups because they use so many online services and social media platforms. Cybercriminals know that young people are often more trusting and less experienced at spotting scams. Today, you'll learn to spot the bait before you bite — and protect yourself from the growing danger of online threats.

📚

Phishing — The Digital Bait
Phishing is a type of cyberattack where criminals send fraudulent emails, text messages (smishing), or phone calls (vishing) designed to trick you into revealing sensitive information. These messages often create a sense of urgency, fear, or excitement to make you act without thinking.
Key Characteristics of Phishing Attempts:
Characteristic What It Looks Like Why It Works
Urgency "Act now!" "Your account will be closed in 24 hours!" Prevents you from thinking critically
Fear "Your account has been hacked!" "We detected suspicious activity!" Makes you panic and act quickly
Too Good to Be True "You won an iPhone!" "You've been selected for a free trip!" Tempts you with excitement
Generic Greetings "Dear Customer," "Dear User," (no name) Sent to thousands at once
Suspicious Links Hover over the link — it doesn't match the real domain Tricks you into visiting fake websites
Poor Grammar/Spelling "We have suspened your account" (misspelled words) Often from non-native speakers
Requests Personal Info "Verify your password," "Confirm your credit card" Legitimate companies NEVER ask for this via email
Types of Phishing:
Type What It Is Example
Email Phishing Fake emails pretending to be from legitimate companies "Your bank account has been frozen. Click here to verify."
Spear Phishing Targeted attacks aimed at specific individuals A scammer pretends to be your teacher and emails you asking for your password
Smishing Phishing via SMS/text messages "Your package could not be delivered. Click this link to reschedule."
Vishing Phishing via voice calls (phone) A scammer calls pretending to be from the tax office demanding payment
Whaling Spear phishing targeting high-profile individuals A scammer impersonates the school principal asking the IT department for access
Real-World Example: A student receives an email that looks exactly like it's from Instagram:
"🚨 URGENT: Your Instagram account will be permanently deleted in 24 hours due to a violation of our Terms of Service. Verify your identity now: http://instagr4m-security.com/verify"
❌ The URL is not Instagram's real domain (instagr4m instead of instagram)
❌ It creates urgency ("24 hours!")
❌ It asks for verification (Instagram never does this via email)
✅ Correct Response: Delete the email. Report it as spam. Never click the link.
Malware & Viruses — The Hidden Invaders
Malware (short for malicious software) is any software intentionally designed to cause damage to a computer, server, client, or computer network. Viruses are a type of malware that spread by attaching themselves to legitimate programs.
Types of Malware:
Type What It Does Example
Virus Attaches to clean files and spreads to other computers Opening an infected email attachment that infects your entire system
Ransomware Locks your files and demands payment to unlock them A pop-up says: "Your files are encrypted. Pay $500 in Bitcoin to recover them."
Spyware Secretly monitors your activity and steals information Records every keystroke to capture passwords and credit card numbers
Trojan Disguises itself as legitimate software but has malicious code Downloading a "free game" that actually gives hackers remote access
Worm Self-replicates and spreads across networks without user interaction A worm spreads through your school's network, infecting every computer
Adware Displays unwanted advertisements and pop-ups Constantly bombarded with ads that slow down your computer
Rootkit Gives hackers deep system access, hiding from antivirus Hackers can control your computer remotely without you knowing
How Malware Spreads:
Method How It Works Prevention
Email Attachments Attachments in phishing emails Never open attachments from unknown senders
Malicious Downloads Downloading software from untrusted sites Only download from official app stores or verified sites
Infected USB Drives Plugging in a USB that contains malware Scan USB drives before opening them
Drive-By Downloads Malware automatically downloads when visiting a compromised website Keep your browser and antivirus updated
Peer-to-Peer Networks Sharing files on P2P networks Avoid illegal file-sharing sites
Symptoms Your Device May Have Malware:
  • ⚠️ Computer runs very slowly
  • ⚠️ Frequent crashes or error messages
  • ⚠️ Pop-up ads appearing constantly
  • ⚠️ Your browser homepage changes unexpectedly
  • ⚠️ New toolbars or extensions you didn't install
  • ⚠️ Files become inaccessible or encrypted
  • ⚠️ You notice unexplained network activity
Real-World Example: A student is playing an online game and sees an ad: "FREE ROBUX — Get unlimited Robux now!" They click the link and download a "Robux generator." Instead of receiving free Robux, they have just installed keylogging spyware that records every keystroke they type — including their passwords, bank details, and personal messages. The hacker now has access to everything.
Fake Websites — The Imposters
Fake websites (also called spoofed or clone websites) are designed to look identical to legitimate websites. Their purpose is to trick you into entering your login credentials, credit card information, or personal data. These sites are often used in phishing campaigns.
How to Spot a Fake Website:
Warning Sign What to Look For Example
Misspelled Domain One character different from the real site g00gle.com (zero instead of o) vs google.com
Unusual Domain Extensions Unknown or weird extensions amazon.shop247.org instead of amazon.com
No HTTPS/Padlock URL starts with http:// not https:// http://paypal-security.com (no padlock)
Design Errors Poor layout, blurry logos, broken links The logo looks slightly stretched or pixelated
Requests Personal Info Asks for passwords, PINs, or credit card numbers "Enter your password to confirm your identity"
Pop-ups Immediately Pop-ups appear as soon as you visit "You are the 1,000th visitor! Claim your prize!"
Common Typosquatting Examples:
Real Website Fake Website (Typosquat) How to Catch It
google.com g00gle.com (zero instead of o) Look closely at the letter 'o'
facebook.com faceb00k.com (zero instead of o) Same trick with the letter 'o'
netflix.com netfIix.com (uppercase I instead of l) The 'l' looks slightly different
youtube.com youtubeb.com (extra 'b') Extra letter at the end
paypal.com paypa1.com (one instead of l) The 'l' is replaced with a number
Real-World Example: A student wants to log into Netflix. They search "Netflix login" on Google and click the first sponsored result. The URL is https://www.netfIix.com (notice the uppercase "I" instead of a lowercase "l"). The site looks identical to Netflix. The student enters their email and password. The site says "Error, please try again." Meanwhile, the hacker has just captured their Netflix credentials and is now using them.
Pop-up Scams — The Annoying Danger
Pop-up scams are fake alerts that appear suddenly on your screen, often claiming that your computer is infected, your files are corrupted, or you've won a prize. These pop-ups are designed to scare you into calling a "tech support" number or clicking a link that installs malware.
Common Pop-up Scams:
Type What It Says The Trap
Fake Virus Alert "⚠️ Your computer is infected! Call 1-800-XXX-XXXX for immediate support." The "support" is a scammer who will ask for money or remote access
Prize Scam "🎉 Congratulations! You've won a free iPhone!" Clicking installs malware or captures your personal data
Browser Hijack "🚨 Your browser is out of date! Click here to update." The "update" is actually malware
Fake Security Scan "🔍 Scanning your computer... Found 34 viruses! Click to remove." The "removal" tool is malware itself
Suspicious Activity "🔒 Suspicious activity detected on your bank account. Click here to secure it." The link goes to a fake bank login page
Red Flags for Pop-up Scams:
  • 🚩 They create urgency — "Do this NOW or you'll lose everything!"
  • 🚩 They use scare tactics — "Your computer is DANGEROUSLY infected!"
  • 🚩 They ask you to call a number — Legitimate companies rarely ask you to call from a pop-up
  • 🚩 They request payment — "Pay $99 now to fix the problem"
  • 🚩 They look unprofessional — Bad grammar, blurry graphics, strange fonts
What to Do When You See a Suspicious Pop-up:
Step Action
1 DON'T CLICK ANYTHING — Do not click "OK," "Cancel," "X," or anything else
2 Close the tab — Use Task Manager (Ctrl+Alt+Delete) to force-close the browser if needed
3 Do not call the number — Never call the number displayed
4 Do not pay money — No legitimate company demands payment via pop-up
5 Report it — Tell a parent, teacher, or IT professional
Real-World Example: A student is playing a free online game. A pop-up suddenly appears:
"⚠️ YOUR PC IS INFECTED WITH 5 VIRUSES! Your personal data is at risk. Call Microsoft Support at 1-800-XXX-XXXX immediately to protect your computer."
The student panics and calls the number. The person on the phone sounds professional and asks for remote access to "fix" the problem. Instead, they steal personal information and demand $300 for a "security subscription" that doesn't exist.
Social Engineering — The Human Hack
Social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people to get them to reveal confidential information or perform actions. Instead of hacking computers, hackers hack humans.
Types of Social Engineering:
Type How It Works Example
Pretexting Creating a false scenario to get information A scammer pretends to be from IT support and asks for your password
Baiting Offering something tempting to get you to act A free USB drive left in the school library that contains malware
Tailgating Following someone into a restricted area A stranger asks you to hold the door open to a secure area
Quid Pro Quo Offering a service in exchange for information "I'll help you with your homework if you give me your Google login"
Impersonation Pretending to be someone trustworthy A scammer pretends to be the principal and emails teachers asking for sensitive data
Real-World Example: A student receives a phone call:
"Hello, this is the IT department. We've detected suspicious activity on your school account. We need you to verify your password so we can secure it."
The student gives their password. The caller is not from IT — they're a social engineer who just stole the student's credentials.

Element Real Email (✅) Phishing Email (❌)
Sender Address admin@schoolname.edu admin@schooI-name.com (notice the uppercase I)

❌ Phishing Email: "URGENT: Your school account has been suspended. Click here to reactivate: https://portaI.schooI-name.com"

Example 3 — Pop-up Scam

Scenario: A student is playing an online game. A pop-up suddenly appears:

"🎉 CONGRATULATIONS! You are the 1,000,000th visitor! You've won a FREE iPhone 16. Click here to claim your prize!"

Why It's a Scam:

  • ❌ Free prizes are almost always scams
  • ❌ Real giveaways don't require you to click random links
  • ❌ It creates excitement to make you act without thinking
  • ❌ "Click here to claim" often leads to malware or phishing sites

✅ Correct Response: Close the pop-up immediately. Do not click anything. Report the site to an adult.

Example 4 — Social Engineering in Action

Scenario: A student receives a text message:

"Hi, this is your friend Sarah. I'm locked out of my Google account and need to verify my identity. Can you send me the verification code you received? I'll send it back to you."

Why It's a Trap:

  • ❌ Your friend would not ask for a verification code via text
  • ❌ The code is for YOUR account — sending it gives them access
  • ❌ Hackers often impersonate friends or family

✅ Correct Response: Do NOT send the code. Call your friend to verify it's really them. Report the message.

Example 5 — Malware Prevention

Scenario: A student downloads a "free" video game from a torrent website. After installation:

  • ⚠️ Computer runs much slower than normal
  • ⚠️ Pop-ups appear even when browser is closed
  • ⚠️ Homepage changed without permission
  • ⚠️ Antivirus software is disabled

What to Do:

  1. Run a full antivirus scan immediately
  2. Disconnect from the internet
  3. Restart the computer in Safe Mode
  4. Run malware removal tools (Malwarebytes, Windows Defender)
  5. Change all passwords from a different, clean device

🛡️ Summary: Online Threats at a Glance

Threat What It Is How to Spot It How to Protect Yourself
Phishing Fake emails/texts to steal info Urgency, generic greetings, suspicious links Never click links, verify sender, report
Malware Malicious software that harms devices Slow computer, pop-ups, crashes Install antivirus, avoid suspicious downloads
Fake Websites Cloned websites that steal data Misspelled domains, no HTTPS, poor design Check URL carefully, look for padlock
Pop-up Scams Fake alerts claiming infection or prize Scare tactics, requests to call numbers Never click, force-close browser
Social Engineering Manipulating people to reveal info Requests for passwords or personal data Always verify identity, be skeptical
