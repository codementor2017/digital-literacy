Social Media Safety

Lock down your digital life

How many of you have posted a photo, checked in at a location, or shared your birthday online? It feels harmless, right? You're just sharing with friends and having fun. But did you know that criminals, stalkers, and even future employers are watching? In Thailand, over 60% of teenagers have shared personal information publicly on social media. A single post with your school name, location, and photo can be used to find you in real life. Predators have used social media posts to track down teenagers. Employers have rejected job applicants because of inappropriate posts from years ago. Colleges have revoked scholarships because of offensive tweets. But here's the good news — you can take control. Most social media platforms have powerful privacy settings that let you decide exactly who sees what. You can protect your personal information, control your reputation, and enjoy social media safely. Let's learn how to lock down your digital life and take back control of your online presence.

📚 What You Need to Know

Privacy Settings — Your First Line of Defense
Privacy settings are the controls on social media platforms that let you choose who can see your posts, personal information, location, and activity. Many platforms default to "Public" settings, which means anyone in the world — including strangers, scammers, and potential employers — can see everything you post. Taking a few minutes to adjust these settings is one of the most important things you can do to protect yourself online.
Key Privacy Settings to Check on Every Platform:
Setting What It Controls Why It Matters
Account Privacy Public vs. Private account Private accounts limit visibility to approved followers only
Post Visibility Who can see each post You can customize who sees different posts (friends, close friends, public)
Story Controls Who can view your stories Hide stories from specific people or share with close friends only
Activity Status Shows when you're online Turn it off to prevent others from knowing when you're active
Tagging Controls Who can tag you in posts Prevent strangers from tagging you in unwanted content
Message Controls Who can send you messages Limit messages to followers or friends only
Data Sharing What data the platform shares with third parties Limit ad targeting and data collection
Step-by-Step Privacy Settings Guide:
Platform Where to Find Settings Key Settings to Change
Instagram Settings → Privacy Account Privacy → Private, Activity Status → Off, Story Controls → Close Friends only
TikTok Settings → Privacy Private Account, Suggest your account to others → Off, Allow others to find me → Off
Facebook Settings & Privacy → Privacy Checkup Who can see your posts → Friends, Who can look you up → Friends
Snapchat Settings → Privacy Controls See My Location → Turn off Ghost Mode, Allow Friends to Find Me → Off
Twitter/X Settings & Support → Privacy & Safety Protect your Tweets → On, Photo tagging → Off
Real-World Example: A student sets their Instagram account to "Private" so only approved followers can see their photos. This prevents strangers from downloading or sharing their images. Additionally, they turn off "Activity Status" so people can't see when they were last online. They also set "Story Controls" to hide their stories from people they don't trust.
Sharing Personal Information — Think Before You Post
Every piece of personal information you share online adds to your digital footprint. Scammers, stalkers, and identity thieves can piece together information from different posts to build a complete profile of you. Even seemingly harmless details like your school name, pet's name, or birthday can be used to guess passwords or answer security questions.
What NOT to Share Online:
Category Examples Why It's Dangerous
Personal Identifiers Full name, date of birth, ID number Identity theft, account takeovers
Location Home address, school name, current location Stalking, physical danger
Contact Info Phone number, email address Harassment, spam, scams
Financial Info Bank details, credit card numbers Fraud, theft
Family Info Family members' names, parents' jobs Social engineering, impersonation
Travel Plans "I'm on vacation for 2 weeks!" Burglary, theft
Private Photos Photos you wouldn't want everyone to see Reputation damage, embarrassment
The "Grandmother Test" Before you post anything, ask yourself:
"Would I be comfortable if my grandmother, my teacher, my future employer, or my future children saw this?"
If the answer is no, don't post it.
Real-World Example: A student posts a photo in their school uniform with the school name tag visible. The photo is public. A stranger now knows their name, their school, and what they look like. This information could be used to find them in real life. The student should have:
• Blurred the school name tag
• Set the post to "Friends Only"
• Avoided posting photos in uniform that identify their school
Friend/Follow Management — Know Who You're Sharing With
Who you accept as a friend or follower on social media determines who can see your posts. Many people accept friend requests from strangers because it feels good to have a larger following. But every new follower is a potential threat. Scammers often create fake accounts to get access to your personal information.
Red Flags for Fake or Suspicious Accounts:
Warning Sign What to Look For
No Profile Photo Default avatar or generic image
Few Posts or Followers New account with very little activity
Oversharing Too eager to message or ask personal questions
Suspicious Username Strange combination of letters and numbers
No Mutual Friends No friends in common
Links in Bio Links to suspicious websites
Requests Money Asking for money or gift cards
Best Practices for Friend/Follow Management:
Practice Why It's Important
Only accept people you know in real life Protects your privacy and safety
Review followers periodically Remove suspicious or inactive accounts
Block and report suspicious accounts Prevents future contact and helps platforms take action
Be wary of "friend of a friend" requests Hackers often use mutual friends as trust signals
Check profiles before accepting Look for red flags
Real-World Example: A student accepts a follower request from an account with 0 posts, a generic profile photo, and no mutual friends. A few days later, the account sends a DM with a link saying: "Check out my new video — you're in it!" The student clicks the link and is directed to a fake login page that steals their Instagram credentials.
Lesson: Don't accept friend requests from strangers. Always check profiles before accepting.
Reporting & Blocking — How to Handle Cyberbullies and Harassment
Social media platforms have systems in place to report and block users who engage in harassment, bullying, or inappropriate behavior. Reporting alerts the platform so they can investigate and take action — from removing content to permanently banning the user. Blocking immediately prevents the user from contacting you.
When to Report and Block:
Situation Example Action
Harassment Someone sends threatening or hateful messages Report → Harassment, Block
Impersonation Someone creates a fake account pretending to be you Report → Impersonation
Bullying Someone posts mean comments or spreads rumors Report → Bullying or Harassment
Inappropriate Content Someone sends explicit or disturbing content Report → Inappropriate Content
Scams/Phishing Someone sends suspicious links or asks for money Report → Scam, Block
Step-by-Step Reporting Guide:
Platform How to Report How to Block
Instagram Tap ... → Report → Choose reason Tap ... → Block
TikTok Tap Share → Report → Choose reason Tap Share → Block
Facebook Tap ... → Find Support or Report Tap ... → Block
Snapchat Press and hold → Report → Choose reason Press and hold → More → Block
Twitter/X Tap ... → Report → Choose reason Tap ... → Block
Real-World Example: A student receives a threatening DM from a classmate. They tap the message → Report → Choose "Harassment" → Block the user. They then screenshot the message and show it to their parents. The classmate's account is temporarily suspended, and the school counselor is informed.
Location Services — Stop Sharing Where You Are
Location tagging on social media shares your physical location with everyone who can see your post. This includes not only your current location but also the places you frequently visit. Posting your location tells people where you are, where you're not, and where you hang out — which can be dangerous for several reasons.
Dangers of Sharing Location:
Risk Example Why It's Dangerous
Stalking Tagging your school location Predators know where to find you
Burglary "I'm on vacation in Phuket!" Thieves know your house is empty
Surveillance Tagging your favorite café People know your daily routines
Harassment Tagging a public event People can find you in real life
Data Collection Location data sold to advertisers Companies build a profile of your habits
Best Practices for Location Safety:
  • ✅ Turn off location tagging for all posts
  • ✅ Don't post your home address or school address
  • ✅ Don't share your daily schedule or routines
  • ✅ Avoid posting "I'm on vacation" until you return
  • ✅ Don't tag your home in any posts
  • ✅ Use "Check In" sparingly and only with close friends
Real-World Example: A student posts a photo at a café and tags the location. A stranger now knows their favorite hangout spot and can easily find them there. A few weeks later, a stranger approaches them at that café and mentions their social media post — a frightening and dangerous situation. The student could have avoided this by turning off location tagging.
Digital Footprint & Reputation on Social Media
Everything you post on social media contributes to your digital footprint — a permanent record of your online activity. Even if you delete a post, it may have been screenshotted, archived, or saved by others. Your social media activity can affect:
• College admissions — Many universities check applicants' social media
• Job applications — 70% of employers check social media before hiring
• Scholarships — Some scholarship committees review online behavior
• Personal relationships — People judge based on what you post
What Your Social Media Posts Say About You:
Type of Post Message It Sends
Positive, helpful posts Mature, responsible, kind
Negative, hateful posts Immature, toxic, unprofessional
Oversharing personal info Poor judgment, naive
Professional content Ambitious, hardworking, intelligent
Activism/social good Caring, engaged, thoughtful
Tips for Building a Positive Reputation:
  • ✅ Post content you'd be proud of — Future employers will see it
  • ✅ Think before you post — "Would I want my family to see this?"
  • ✅ Delete old, embarrassing posts — Clean up your digital footprint
  • ✅ Use privacy settings — Limit who can see your content
  • ✅ Create a positive presence — Post about your achievements, hobbies, and interests
  • ✅ Be kind online — Your comments reflect your character
Real-World Example: A student is applying to a prestigious university. The admissions officer searches their name online and finds:
✅ A LinkedIn profile with volunteer work and academic achievements
❌ A public Twitter account with political arguments and inappropriate jokes
❌ A Facebook post with offensive language from 2 years ago
The student missed out on admission because of the negative posts. The lesson: clean up your digital footprint before applying to anything important.
Age Restrictions — Why They Matter
Most social media platforms have a minimum age requirement of 13 (under COPPA in the US) or 16 (under GDPR in Europe). These restrictions exist to protect young users from inappropriate content, data collection, and online predators. When you lie about your age to create an account, you are violating the platform's terms of service and putting yourself at risk.
Minimum Age Requirements:
Platform Minimum Age Why It Matters
Instagram 13 Protects children from predators and inappropriate content
TikTok 13 Limited features for underage users (different data rules)
Facebook 13 COPPA compliance
Snapchat 13 Protects against location tracking and strangers
YouTube 13 Limited content and restricted features for underage users
Twitter/X 13 Data protection and age-appropriate content
Why You Shouldn't Lie About Your Age:
  • ❌ It violates Terms of Service — your account could be suspended
  • ❌ You'll see content not meant for your age group
  • ❌ Your data may be used in ways you don't understand
  • ❌ You miss out on safety features designed for younger users
  • ❌ Legal consequences for platforms that collect data from underage users

Example 3 — Reporting Harassment

Scenario: A student receives a threatening DM on Instagram:

"I know where you live. I'm going to come to your school tomorrow."

Step-by-Step Response:

Step Action Explanation
1 Screenshot the message Evidence for reporting and authorities
2 Do NOT reply Engaging can escalate the situation
3 Report the message Tap message → Report → Harassment/Bullying
4 Block the user Tap ... → Block → Confirm
5 Tell a trusted adult Tell parents, teacher, or school counselor
6 Save the evidence Store screenshots in a safe place
7 If threatened, contact authorities If it's a serious threat, call the police

Why This Works: Reporting alerts Instagram to investigate. Blocking prevents further contact. Telling an adult ensures someone knows what's happening. Screenshots provide evidence.

Example 5 — Friend/Follow Management Audit

How to Audit Your Followers:

Step Action
1 Scroll through your follower list
2 For each person, ask: "Do I know them in real life?"
3 If NO, remove them by clicking Remove or Block
4 For people you do know, ask: "Is their account legitimate?"
5 If suspicious, check for red flags (no posts, no profile photo, strange username)
6 For suspicious accounts, Block and Report

Red Flags Checklist:

Red Flags
  • Generic profile photo (not a real person)
  • No posts or very few posts
  • No mutual friends
  • Strange username (random numbers/letters)
  • Follows hundreds but has few followers
  • Sends unsolicited DMs

If any of these apply, remove the follower.
