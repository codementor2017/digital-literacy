Week 1

Safe Browsing & Strong Passwords

Build your unbreakable digital fortress

Imagine you have a diary with all your secrets — your thoughts, your passwords, your private conversations, and even your family's financial information. Would you leave it unlocked on a park bench? Of course not! But every single day, millions of people leave their digital lives unprotected with weak passwords like '123456' or 'password'. In 2023, the most common password worldwide was still '123456' — hackers cracked it in less than one second using automated tools. Your password is the first lock protecting your email, school accounts, social media, online banking, and even your family's digital identity. Once a hacker gets in, they can steal your personal data, impersonate you, or even lock you out of your own accounts. Today, we're going to learn how to build an unbreakable digital fortress that keeps your online life safe and secure.

📚 Topics to Cover

Topic 1: Creating Strong Passwords
A strong password is your first line of defense against unauthorized access. It should be:
Criteria Requirement Why It Matters
Length At least 12 characters Longer passwords are exponentially harder to crack
Uppercase Letters At least 1 (A-Z) Increases complexity
Lowercase Letters At least 1 (a-z) Increases complexity
Numbers At least 1 (0-9) Increases complexity
Special Characters At least 1 (!@#$%^&*) Makes brute-force attacks much harder
Avoid Common Words No dictionary words, names, or birthdays Hackers use dictionary attacks
Uniqueness Different password for every account If one gets hacked, others stay safe
Common Password Mistakes to Avoid:
  • ❌ Using your name or birthdate (John2005)
  • ❌ Using simple sequences (123456, qwerty)
  • ❌ Using dictionary words (password, sunshine)
  • ❌ Using the same password everywhere
  • ❌ Writing passwords on sticky notes or in plain text files
How Hackers Crack Passwords:
Attack Method How It Works How to Defend
Brute Force Tries every possible combination Use long passwords (12+ characters)
Dictionary Attack Uses a list of common words Avoid dictionary words and names
Rainbow Table Attack Uses precomputed hash tables Use random characters and 2FA
Phishing Tricks you into revealing your password Never click suspicious links
Credential Stuffing Uses passwords from other breaches Use unique passwords for each site
Real-World Example: ❌ Weak: John2005 (cracked in 0.2 seconds — contains a name + year)  |  ✅ Strong: J0hn!L0v3s#C0d1ng (would take 1,000+ years to crack — mix of cases, symbols, numbers, and personal meaning)
Pro Tip: Use a Passphrase Instead of a single word, use a random sentence or phrase:
Example: Purple!Dragon$FlyHigh#2025
Easier to remember, harder to crack
Topic 2: Password Managers
A password manager is a tool that generates, stores, and manages all your passwords in one secure, encrypted location. You only need to remember ONE master password — the manager handles the rest.
Popular Password Managers:
Tool Platform Free Version Key Feature
Bitwarden All platforms ✅ Yes Open-source, fully featured
Dashlane All platforms ⚠️ Limited Dark web monitoring
1Password All platforms ❌ Paid Travel mode, secure vaults
LastPass All platforms ⚠️ Limited Emergency access
KeePass Desktop ✅ Yes Offline, open-source
How Password Managers Work:
  1. You create one master password (very strong!)
  2. The manager encrypts all your passwords with strong encryption (AES-256)
  3. You log in with your master password to access your vault
  4. The manager auto-fills passwords on websites
  5. It can generate random, strong passwords for new accounts
Benefits of Using a Password Manager:
  • 🔐 Generate strong passwords — no need to think of them yourself
  • 🔐 Store unlimited passwords — no more forgetting
  • 🔐 Auto-fill — saves time and prevents typos
  • 🔐 Security alerts — some notify you of data breaches
  • 🔐 Cross-device sync — access on phone, computer, tablet
  • 🔐 Encrypted storage — even the company can't read your passwords
Real-World Example: A student has 20+ online accounts. Instead of using the same password everywhere, they use Bitwarden to generate and store unique passwords like:
G00gl3!Cl@ss#2025 C@nv@$ketch! Scr@tch#C0d3 Quiz!et#Fun@ M!necr@ft#B1ock
Now they only need to remember one master password to access all of them.
Topic 3: Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)
Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) adds a second layer of security to your accounts. Even if someone steals or guesses your password, they still can't get in without the second factor.
The Three Types of Authentication Factors:
Factor Type What It Is Examples
Something you KNOW Information you remember Password, PIN, security question
Something you HAVE A physical device Phone, security key, smart card
Something you ARE Biometric data Fingerprint, face scan, voice
Types of 2FA Methods:
Method How It Works Security Level Pros & Cons
SMS Code Text message with a 6-digit code Medium Easy, but vulnerable to SIM-swapping
Authenticator App App generates a 6-digit code High Works offline, more secure than SMS
Email Code Code sent to your email Low-Medium If email is hacked, 2FA is compromised
Hardware Key Physical USB key (YubiKey) Very High Most secure, but costs money
Backup Codes One-time use codes provided when setting up 2FA High Save them in a safe place
How to Enable 2FA on Popular Platforms:
Platform How to Enable
Google/Gmail Settings → Security → 2-Step Verification
Instagram Settings → Security → Two-Factor Authentication
Facebook Settings → Security and Login → Use two-factor authentication
Microsoft/Outlook Security → Advanced security options → Two-step verification
Apple ID Settings → Password & Security → Two-Factor Authentication
Real-World Example: A student logs into their school email. After entering their password, they receive a text message with a 6-digit code. Without that code, a hacker cannot access the account — even if they guessed or stole the password.
Topic 4: Recognizing Secure Websites
Not all websites are safe. When you enter personal information like passwords, credit card numbers, or addresses, you need to ensure the website is secure.
How to Identify a Secure Website:
Indicator What to Look For What It Means
HTTPS URL starts with https:// Encrypted connection — data is scrambled
Padlock Icon 🔒 in the address bar Connection is secure
Certificate Click padlock → "Certificate is valid" Website identity is verified
Green Bar Some browsers show a green bar Extended Validation — high trust
What to Avoid:
  • ❌ HTTP sites (no padlock) — never enter personal info
  • ❌ Misspelled domains (g00gle.com instead of google.com)
  • ❌ Suspicious pop-ups asking for personal info
  • ❌ Websites with outdated security certificates
Real-World Example: Before buying something online, always check if the URL starts with https://. If it's http:// (no "s"), your credit card information could be intercepted by hackers on the same network. Always look for the padlock icon 🔒 before entering any personal information.

Example 5 — Phishing Attempt with Fake Website

Scenario: A student receives an email:

"Your Instagram account has been compromised. Click here to verify your identity: http://instagr4m-security.com"
  • ❌ URL is not Instagram's real domain (instagr4m instead of instagram)
  • ❌ No https:// (no padlock)
  • ❌ Urgency tactic ("your account has been compromised")

✅ Correct Response: Delete the email. Never click the link.
